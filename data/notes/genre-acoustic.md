What these genres share is not instrumentation but recording philosophy: the room is part of
the record. You can hear the space, the breath, the finger noise, the slight timing drift of
humans playing together. That is what acousticness measures, and it is why a drum machine
playing a folk pattern still does not sound like folk.

Folk is sparse and lyric-led, usually 70-95 BPM, built around a single voice and a single
instrument; it reads as intimate and often melancholy. Jazz in a listening context is
usually the quieter end - a trio at 80-100 BPM, warm and conversational, which is why it
dominates cafes. Classical spans everything, but a solo nocturne is the lowest-energy,
highest-acousticness material in most catalogues and the obvious answer to a request for
quiet. Country and gospel both carry strong vocal narrative; gospel is distinctive for
being communal rather than individual, with the highest valence of any of these.

For a system, the useful consequence is that acousticness is a better discriminator than
genre within this family. A listener who wants something quiet and real is describing an
acousticness above 0.8, and will accept folk, classical or jazz interchangeably.
